Systems Design

Brand and UI reference

The design system behind the Systems Design reference, written down so it can be applied to another project without rebuilding the decisions. Every rule below is one that already earns its place in a shipped page.

01Mark

A red square holding the wordmark, bottom-left, wrapping inside its own padding. The square is the constant; the words change per project. It descends from Swiss corporate design of the sixties, where a solid colour field carried the name rather than sitting beside it.

Favicon

The same square rendered as an isometric cube, three faces in one red family: base red front, a lifted tint on top, a shaded tone on the right, hairline edges in the background colour. It stays legible at 32px, which flat wordmarks do not.

Cube faces: #C62C27 front, #E8564F top, #8E1F1B right. Tile #0C1A2F, radius 10 on a 64 grid.

Do

  • Keep the wordmark inside the square, never beside it.
  • Let it wrap to two or three lines. That is the look.
  • Leave clear space of at least half the square's width.

Don't

  • Centre the text or add a second colour inside the square.
  • Round the corners beyond 2px.
  • Shrink type to force a single line.

02Colour

Six colours. One accent does all the work, one secondary carries data, everything else is structure. The discipline is that red means action or emphasis and nothing else, so it never has to compete with itself.

Prussian Blue#0C1A2F Dark ground and dark-theme inset. Also the favicon tile.
Alabaster Grey#E3E4E8 Primary text on dark. Never a background on its own now that light mode is white.
Flag Red#C62C27 The one accent. Active state, focus ring, checked state, the mark. Roughly 5% of any screen.
Cool Steel#9098A6 Labels, meta, inactive controls, monospace eyebrows.
Mauve Shadow#745863 Red's quiet partner. Counts, list markers, borders that hint without shouting.
Signal Teal#4E9DAE Second data colour. Charts, diagram stores, positive lists. Darkens to #2B6E7D on white.

Usage rules

Do

  • Use red for one thing per view: the active or the actionable.
  • Pair red with teal when two series must be told apart.
  • Encode meaning in position and label as well as colour.
  • Darken teal on light backgrounds. The dark value fails contrast on white.

Don't

  • Introduce a third accent. Six is the whole palette.
  • Use Prussian Blue as a chart colour on a Prussian Blue ground.
  • Rely on colour alone to separate categories.
  • Tint whole surfaces to group things. Use borders and spacing.

Two themes, two strategies

Dark mode separates surfaces by tint: each layer is a step lighter than the one behind it. Light mode is uniformly white, so separation comes from elevation and border instead. Do not port the tint approach onto white; it produces grey soup.

03Typography

IBM Plex Sans and IBM Plex Mono. Plex was drawn for IBM, so it carries enterprise-technical association honestly rather than by decoration. The mono is not ornamental: it marks machine-side content, codes, counts and labels, so the reader can tell data from prose at a glance.

Rules

Do

  • Sentence case everywhere, including labels and buttons.
  • Cap measure at 64 to 74 characters for prose.
  • Use weight 450 and 550 where 400 is thin and 600 is heavy. Plex supports it.
  • Negative tracking above 1.5rem only. Small text needs its space.

Don't

  • Set eyebrows in all caps with wide tracking. Mono at small size does the job.
  • Use mono for prose, only for codes, counts, labels and code.
  • Go below 0.75rem for anything a reader must act on.
  • Mix in a second display face.

05Elevation and focus

Three states only. Resting, raised, focused. Focus is the one place the accent becomes a glow, because it answers a question the user is actively asking: which thing am I about to scroll or type into.

Shadow values

StateDarkLight
Resting0 1px 2px rgba(0,0,0,.34)0 1px 2px rgba(12,26,47,.05)
Raisedtint step on --surface-20 5px 16px -4px rgba(12,26,47,.16)
Focused0 0 0 1px var(--accent), 0 0 22px 1px rgba(198,44,39,.34)

Focus follows a click, not only a tab press. Clicking a scrollable card gives it the ring and moves keyboard focus into its body, so the wheel and the arrow keys act on the thing the user just pointed at.

07Controls

Controls are outlined, not filled. Filled buttons would compete with the accent, which is reserved for state. The active state is a red border plus a wash, never a solid red fill except on the mark itself.

Buttons and filter chips

Icon buttons

38px square, 1px border, 5px radius. Utility buttons hover to --text; buttons that leave the page hover to --accent.

Checkboxes

Round, drawn from scratch rather than accent-color, so the tick and ring are controllable. Checked means reviewed, not deleted, so the text brightens rather than crossing out.

Input

Search matches terms, aliases and body text. Aliases matter: without them, "idempotent" fails to find "Idempotency".

08Content blocks

Five recurring blocks. Each has one job, and the visual treatment encodes that job rather than decorating it.

Takeaway — one memorable line, front of card

Structure beats accountability. Find the loop.

Accent wash, 3px left rule. One sentence, never two.

Ask — a diagnostic question, back of card

Where does this output become someone else's input?

Inset background, italic. Always answerable in a meeting.

Field example — evidence

An integration team held the top incident count for two years. A correlation identifier reattributed most of those incidents to three source systems.

Accent left rule, no background. Concrete and specific.

09Motion

Motion answers an action. Nothing moves on load, nothing loops, nothing draws attention to itself. Four durations cover the whole system.

UseDurationEasing
Hover, colour, opacity.15sease
Disclosure, fade in or out.18s to .2sease
Card flip.5scubic-bezier(.2,.7,.3,1)
Chart redraw.45slibrary default

Every transition sits behind @media (prefers-reduced-motion: reduce), which disables all of them. A flip that cannot animate still flips.

10Voice

Plain declarative sentences that a knowledgeable colleague would actually say. The content earns attention by being specific, not by being emphatic.

Do

  • Lead with the claim, then support it.
  • Use concrete numbers and named failure modes.
  • Write titles as assertions: "Coupling is the thing you are actually designing".
  • Say what to do, and what it costs.

Don't

  • Em dashes. Use a comma, a colon or a full stop.
  • Marketing register: leverage, unlock, seamless, robust, best-in-class.
  • "Not X, but Y" constructions and rhetorical questions as headings.
  • Hedging that removes the claim entirely.
